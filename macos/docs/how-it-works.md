# How it works

`Sources/CircuitKit` is the simulation engine, written for live stepping and tested on its own (`swift test`). It uses modified nodal analysis, like JSpice and SPICE. `Sources/JSpiceAutomation` holds the automation tools and the MCP server, `Sources/jspice-mcp` the stdio server, and `Sources/JSpice` the SwiftUI and AppKit app; the layout of the package and how it is built and tested are in [CONTRIBUTING.md](../../CONTRIBUTING.md).

## Nodes and the sparse solve

- Wires and closed switches merge their ends into one node (a 3PDT footswitch each pole with its throw, a switched jack its tip with its normal lug or the plug); their currents (for the dots) are recovered afterwards from Kirchhoff's current law.
- The equations are solved by a sparse elimination planned once per circuit: a Markowitz ordering with threshold pivoting, the unknowns no nonlinear part touches first. Their block is factored once for each base matrix (a new time step or switch state; those for each state of the chips' pins and the bus parts are kept, as an I²C bus goes back and forth between the same few at nearly every step), and what it adds to the rest kept. Newton-Raphson then restamps and refactors only the nonlinear block, kept whole, with a few pivot orders that it tries in turn, so a part swinging between two states (an op-amp into and out of saturation) switches between two orders made once.
- Each step's Newton-Raphson starts from the solution extrapolated, unknown by unknown, along whichever came nearer to where that unknown ended last time: the straight line through the last two substeps, or the parabola through the last three (a signal moving smoothly is followed along the parabola, one nearly still or turning sharply keeps to the line). That saves an iteration while a signal moves.
- Newton-Raphson stops as soon as an iteration moves the solution by less than a millionth: it converges quadratically, so the solution is then within about 10⁻¹¹ of the answer, and the iteration that would only confirm it is skipped. VCAs are solved with the circuit (in one iteration where they are its only nonlinear parts).
- Each step is cheap: parameters are read once when the circuit loads, only the parts that need it are visited, and Newton-Raphson reuses its buffers.

## Time steps

- Capacitors and inductors use second-order Gear (BDF2) companion models: an LC circuit keeps oscillating at the right frequency, and unlike the trapezoidal rule a sudden step does not make currents ring from one time step to the next.
- Each time step is taken in substeps where it needs them. The local error of every capacitor voltage and inductor current is estimated from the third divided difference of its last four values. Where it would be more than 0.1 % (plus 0.1 mV or 0.1 µA), as at an edge or where a diode turns on, and where Newton-Raphson does not converge, the step is solved again in halves, quarters, down to a 64th. The substeps grow back by doubling where the error allows, and BDF2 takes variable-step coefficients. Where nothing changes quickly a step is a single solve, as before. With sound on, substeps follow only Newton-Raphson's failures, as the sound sets its own step by how fast the computer keeps up.

## Nonlinear parts and convergence

Diodes, Zener diodes, LEDs, bipolar transistors, MOSFETs (level 1), op-amps (an internal stage with a single pole at the gain-bandwidth, slew-rate limited, ahead of a smooth output limit), OTAs, JFETs and analog switches are solved with Newton-Raphson and junction voltage limiting at every time step: a junction's step up is taken through the logarithm, as SPICE takes it, to the voltage at which it carries the current its linearisation gives, and so is a conducting junction's step down (Newton-Raphson's own step down would turn it off a factor of e an iteration, a dozen iterations for an LED going dark).

When a circuit snaps from one state to another, like the two transistors of a flip-flop changing over, Newton is guided to the new solution by gmin stepping: the junctions are briefly shunted and the shunts stepped down to nothing. In a circuit with behavioural sources, where Newton's steps stop shrinking without a junction limiting them (the sharp corner of a TABLE, LIMIT or clamp, which a full step can jump across and back), it is damped: each such iteration halves the step it takes.

## Device models

- **Bipolar transistors** are SPICE's Gummel-Poon model with ngspice's equations, so a manufacturer's `.model` card imported from a netlist (or typed into the inspector's SPICE Model Parameters) behaves as it does in ngspice: forward and reverse gain and emission, both Early voltages, high-level injection (IKF, IKR, NKF), the base-emitter and base-collector leakage currents, a base resistance that falls with the base current (RB, RBM, IRB), collector and emitter resistances on internal nodes, depletion capacitances split between the internal and external base (XCJC), a transit time that rises with the current and the collector voltage (TF, XTF, VTF, ITF) and the reverse transit time, with the band gap and the saturation current's and the gain's temperature exponents. Its parameters' defaults are SPICE's, so a transistor given only a gain and a saturation current is the Ebers-Moll transistor. `crosscheck.py --devices` sweeps transistors with test cards using every one of these parameters through ngspice's `.dc` (base-emitter voltage, and collector-emitter voltage from reverse through saturation, at 27 °C and 70 °C), and `DeviceModelTests` requires JSpice's currents to match within 10 parts per million; the transient and small-signal cross-checks include an amplifier, a saturating switch and a PNP stage with such cards, and the noise check their resistances' thermal noise.
- **MOSFETs** are SPICE's level 1 with ngspice's equations: the channel in both directions with channel-length modulation and the body effect, drain and source resistances on internal nodes, the bulk junctions (the body diode from source to drain) with their depletion charges, Meyer's gate capacitances (averaged over each step as ngspice does) with the overlap capacitances, and the temperature of its transconductance, threshold and junctions. A card's process parameters (TOX, U0, NSUB, LD, CJ, JS, RSH) are worked out with the transistor's W, L, areas and squares when a netlist is read. `crosscheck.py --devices` sweeps NMOS and PMOS test cards through ngspice's `.dc` (gate voltage through the threshold at 27 °C and 70 °C, drain voltage from the body diode through inverse mode into saturation), within 10 parts per million in `DeviceModelTests`, and the transient and small-signal checks add an NMOS switch through its Miller plateau and a common-source stage out to 100 MHz.
- **JFETs** are SPICE's (ngspice's level 1, the Shichman-Hodges model with the University of Sydney's doping-tail parameter B): the channel current in both directions with channel-length modulation, the two gate junctions (which conduct when the gate is driven past its source or drain), drain and source resistances on internal nodes, the gate-source and gate-drain depletion capacitances, and the temperature of its threshold (TCV), transconductance (BEX or BETATCE) and gate current. A drawn JFET is set by its pinch-off voltage and IDSS; a card's BETA is worked out from them. A P-channel JFET (a PJF card, as in the input stage of a TL07x macromodel) is the same equations with every voltage and current reversed, its VTO negative as SPICE writes it, and the device check sweeps the test card as a P-JFET too. `crosscheck.py --devices` sweeps a test card through ngspice's `.dc` (gate voltage from cut-off into gate conduction, at 27 °C and 70 °C, and drain voltage from inverse mode through saturation), within 10 parts per million in `DeviceModelTests`, and the transient and small-signal checks add a stage with such a card driven from cut-off into gate conduction and out to its Miller pole.
- **Diodes, Zener diodes and LEDs** are all SPICE's diode with ngspice's equations: the junction with its emission coefficient, a series resistance on an internal node, reverse breakdown matched (as ngspice matches it) to the current IBV at BV with its own emission coefficient NBV, forward and reverse high-injection knees, depletion and diffusion charge, and the temperature. A Zener is a diode whose card has a breakdown voltage; an LED's saturation current comes from its colour unless its card gives one. `crosscheck.py --devices` sweeps test cards through ngspice's `.dc` (forward with the series resistance and knee, reverse into breakdown, a Zener past its knee, an LED both ways, and at 70 °C), within 10 parts per million in `DeviceModelTests`, and the transient check adds a fast rectifier and a Zener regulator with such cards.

What these models leave out is in [limitations.md](limitations.md#device-models).

## Controlled sources

Controlled sources are behavioural sources: a voltage or a current set by an expression of the voltages at up to eight sense pins and the currents through named voltage sources (inside a block, the block's own first), with SPICE's operators, functions (abs, sqrt, exp, ln, log10, the trigonometric and hyperbolic ones, min, max, pow, pwr, pwrs, limit, u, uramp, sgn, if, table), time and temperature.

- Each expression is differentiated symbolically once, so Newton-Raphson gets exact slopes, and evaluating it allocates nothing.
- One whose slopes are constants (a gain, POLY of the first degree; a maker's model has dozens) is a linear part, stamped once: TI's GWL models' nonlinear blocks shrink from about 510 unknowns to 225.
- An expression's decisions (comparisons, IF, logical operators, u, sgn, floor, ceil) are held through each solve where it started, so its equations are smooth through Newton-Raphson, and the solve is done again with them made where it ended if one has changed (a comparator switching); a comparator with nothing between its two states that chatters about its threshold (as TI's models sense an output's overload) is left after a few rounds, its substep not refined for an error that means nothing there. ngspice stops on such a comparator ("timestep too small").

`crosscheck.py --netlists` runs decks as written in ngspice: E, F, G and H with gains and POLY, B sources, TABLE and VALUE, and a Boyle-style op-amp macromodel whose F and H read a source inside its subcircuit; and a library file of JFET-input op-amps read with `.lib` and `.include`, one instance given its own `PARAMS:`, its output limited by a `.func`, with P-JFETs, a diode and a transistor scaled by their areas, and switches and model resistors (ngspice has no VSWITCH or ISWITCH, so its deck spells their resistance as B sources; and ngspice rounds a TABLE's corners, a quadratic over a tenth of the shorter segment on either side, where PSpice's TABLE, which makers' models are written for, and JSpice's are sharp, so its deck spells the table with sharp corners). The comparison starts 1 % into each run: from rest, the first steps of a stiff power-up depend on each simulator's step; `SpiceNetlistCrossCheckTests` imports the same text and requires each net to follow ngspice within 1 % of its range.

## Makers' models at audio rate

A maker's op-amp model ([spice.md](spice.md#makers-models)) has a nonlinear block of a few hundred unknowns, and most of the work on it can be skipped without changing the answer (in a circuit of transistors, diodes and built-in op-amps, whose slopes move at every iteration, keeping and comparing for that costs more than it saves, so only circuits with behavioural sources do it):

- While the block's entries stay within 10⁻⁷ of those it was last factored with (against the largest in their row), Newton-Raphson keeps the factors and solves with them from the present residual (the chord method), which converges to the same solution; the last few factorings are kept too, so a comparator in the model that flips and flips back takes up the factoring it had.
- An iteration whose block stamps exactly as the one before solved it (within 10⁻¹²) has that solution and ends without a solve, and the next step's first iteration starts from those stamps; its second evaluates every part at its solution and confirms it, or Newton-Raphson starts again from the prediction.
- A behavioural source whose inputs have not moved, or one made of straight lines between its decisions (LIMIT, IF, TABLE, as makers' models are, and a switch held off or on) whose inputs have moved less than half the distance to its nearest decision, is stamped as it last was, as SPICE bypasses a device: within that distance its stamp is exactly the same.

TI's LM358 model, whose output stage switches itself off and on again about its crossover, used to take a dozen iterations at each crossing and now takes a few more than the rest of the cycle. The engine sees the stage's decisions go round in a cycle and keeps them as held; it starts a crossing that does not converge again from before its dead zone; it remembers which crossings went which way, so that the next of the same kind starts there at once; and it starts the step after a crossing from its solution rather than from a guess drawn through the bend. The timings are in [spice.md](spice.md#running-an-op-amp-on-its-makers-model).

## Switching parts, memristors, tubes and transformers

- 555 timers, Schmitt inverters and the CMOS logic parts switch between discrete states. A substep in which one switches is solved again with the new state, so timing does not lag by a step. The counters and flip-flops act on their clock's edges as the substeps find them, and a substep that is thrown away takes their state back with it.
- Memristors follow a threshold switching model (on resistance, off resistance, on and off thresholds, switching time) like the MSS models in JSpice.
- Tubes are solved like the transistors, with Newton-Raphson and voltage limiting (the grid in steps of 2 V, the plate and screen in steps of 50 V, as they swing over hundreds). Tubes' electrode capacitances are put in the same way as a transformer's parts.
- A transformer is simulated as exactly what two coupled windings are: its primary resistance, a leakage inductance (1 − k²)·Lp, a magnetising inductance k²·Lp across an ideal transformer of ratio n/k, and its secondary resistance, put in when the circuit is flattened like a block's parts. The ideal transformer is one more row in the equations: the secondary is a voltage source of n/k times the primary's voltage, and the primary carries n/k times its current.
- A core can saturate (a transformer's volt-seconds, an inductor's saturation current): its current is then a function of its flux λ, i(λ) = λ/Lsat − (1/Lsat − 1/L0)·λsat·tanh(λ/λsat), so the inductance is L0 while the flux is small and falls to Lsat past λsat, which bends a bass note's peaks and draws a mains transformer's inrush; it is solved as the inductor at Lsat with the core's current on top, a nonlinear part of the flux the inductor's current carries.
- The cross-check includes an overdriven 12AX7 stage, a 6L6GC into its output transformer, a mains transformer and rectifier, against ngspice running Koren's equations and coupled inductors (`K`), and an inductor and a mains transformer driven into saturation (their flux integrated by a capacitor and their current a B source in ngspice).

## Blocks

A block part carries its circuit. Before simulating, each copy's parts are put into the circuit after its own parts, moved off to one side of the drawing. Their ids are made from the block's and their own, so state survives edits. Their names are prefixed with the block's ("X1.R2"), and inside net labels get the copy's own names. Each port becomes a wire to its pin. The engine then sees one flat circuit: blocks cost nothing beyond their parts, and a knob inside a block is a parameter change like any other.

## Small-signal analysis

Small-signal (AC) analysis reuses Newton-Raphson's matrix at the present solution: it holds every diode's, transistor's and chip's slope at the operating point. Capacitors and inductors are taken out and put back as jωC and 1/jωL at each frequency. Parts with dynamics of their own are put back as transfer functions: an op-amp's internal pole, an AS3320's four stages with their feedback (each stage's pole set by how far its soft saturation is driven), a delay line's e^(−jωT) and a PT2399's two clock-tracking poles. The complex equations are solved by sparse-aware elimination at every frequency, around 200 frequencies in a few milliseconds for a typical circuit.

`crosscheck.py --ac` records ngspice's operating point and `.ac` sweep of fifteen circuits: RC and RLC, a Zener's ripple, a common-emitter and a JFET stage, a TL072 inverting amplifier out to its bandwidth, a Sallen-Key low-pass, a 12AX7 stage out to its Miller roll-off, a 6L6GC through its output transformer, the Fuzz Face and overdrive examples, an NPN and a PNP stage with whole Gummel-Poon cards out to 100 MHz, and a JFET and a MOSFET stage with whole cards. `SmallSignalTests` requires JSpice to match within 0.001 dB and 0.01°; it does to better than 0.0001 dB and 0.001°, since the equations are the same and nothing is integrated. The chips ngspice has no model of (the filter, VCA, delay lines and the LM13700 filter) are checked against JSpice's own transient simulation driven with a small sine.

## Loop gain

The Loop Probe's loop gain T ([guide.md](guide.md#scopes-and-analyses)) comes from Middlebrook's double injection on the circuit linearised where it settles: a voltage injected in series at the break gives Tv = −v(in) / v(out), a current injected into it Ti = i(in) / i(out), and T = (Tv Ti − 1) / (Tv + Ti + 2), exact with loading on both sides of the break (a transconductance between two resistors reads gm (Rx ‖ Ry), where a voltage injection alone reads gm Rx + Rx / Ry). The phase margin is taken where |T| falls through 1 (or rises through it, below an AC-coupled loop's band, as the lead left), the gain margin where T's phase crosses −180° nearest crossover; an op-amp closing its loop through two RC sections reads both where the formula puts them.

## Noise

Noise analysis carries every resistor's thermal noise, every junction's shot noise, the channel noise of field-effect transistors, tubes' noise and op-amps' input noise to the output through the linearised circuit, as SPICE's `.noise` does. One solve of the transposed equations per frequency gives how a current anywhere reaches the output, so all the sources cost one solve. It matches ngspice's `.noise` within 0.01 dB. There is no flicker (1/f) noise.

## Sound and pacing

- With sound on, a copy of the simulator runs on its own thread, four steps per sample at the output's rate (fewer if the circuit is too heavy), filling about 50 ms of sound ahead; the window's simulator takes on its state at each display frame to draw it. `jspice-mcp --benchmark` times every example at 48 kHz.
- `Pacing` estimates the circuit's time constants and periods to pick the speed and time step; `Simulator.advance` stops at a per-frame compute budget, so the app stays responsive however heavy the circuit is.

## Checked against ngspice

`tools/spice-reference/crosscheck.py` writes SPICE decks with JSpice's own device equations for twenty-nine circuits: RC and RLC, a rectifier, a 1N4001's reverse recovery, a Zener regulator, an LED, a common-emitter amplifier (at 27 °C and at 70 °C), the astable blinker, a CMOS inverter, a JFET, op-amps, the triangle LFO, an overdriven 12AX7 stage, a 6L6GC into its output transformer, a mains transformer and rectifier, the Fuzz Face and overdrive examples, the amplifier (cold and hot), a saturating switch and a PNP stage with whole Gummel-Poon cards, a rectifier and a Zener regulator with whole diode cards, a JFET stage and a MOSFET switch with whole cards. It runs them with tight tolerances and records the waveforms (`Tests/CircuitKitTests/Fixtures/spice-reference.json`). `SpiceCrossCheckTests` simulates the same circuits at the step JSpice picks and at a tenth of it, and fails beyond 5 % of a waveform's range, or of an oscillator's period and swing. CI prints the table.

The other modes of the same script record the small-signal, device and netlist fixtures described above (`--ac`, `--devices`, `--netlists`); how to run it is in [CONTRIBUTING.md](../../CONTRIBUTING.md#reference-tools-and-fixtures). The microcontroller emulators are checked against simavr, rp2040js and QEMU ([microcontrollers.md](microcontrollers.md)).
